Kelime seçimi

Fiil çekimi (Präsens)

Almanca fiiller sözlükte mastar hâliyle durur: lernen, wohnen. Çekim yaparken sondaki -en (bazen sadece -n) atılır, kalan köke kişiye göre bir ek gelir. Düzenli fiillerin hepsi aynı kalıbı izler:

ichlernewirlernen
dulernstihrlernt
er/sie/eslerntsie/Sielernen

Kalıbı bozan birkaç küçük yazım kuralı var. Bunlar telaffuzu kolaylaştırmak için:

  • Kök -t / -d ile bitiyorsa du, er ve ihr'de araya bir e giriyor: du arbeitest, er findet. Yoksa „arbeitst“ gibi söylenmesi zor bir yığın oluşurdu.
  • Kök -s / -ß / -z ile bitiyorsa du'da -st değil sadece -t geliyor: du heißt, du tanzt.
  • -eln ile biten fiillerde ich'te e düşüyor: ich sammle. -ern ile bitenlerde çoğulda sadece -n geliyor: wir feiern.
  • Ayrılan fiiller (listede „|“ ile gösteriliyor, an|rufen gibi): ön ek cümlenin sonuna gidiyor: Ich rufe dich an.

Düzensiz fiillerde değişiklik çoğunlukla sadece du ve er/sie/es'te oluyor; ich ve çoğul biçimler düzenli kalıyor. Kökteki sesli harf değişiyor: sprechen → du sprichst, lesen → er liest, fahren → du fährst. sein, haben, werden, wissen ve modal fiiller (können, müssen, wollen, mögen, möchten) ise kendi kalıplarını izliyor; bunları ayrı ayrı ezberlemek gerekiyor.

Verb conjugation (Präsens)

German verbs are listed in the dictionary in the infinitive: lernen, wohnen. To conjugate, you drop the final -en (sometimes just -n) and add an ending to the stem that matches the person. All regular verbs follow the same pattern:

ichlernewirlernen
dulernstihrlernt
er/sie/eslerntsie/Sielernen

English only adds an ending in one place (he learns); German marks every person. A few small spelling rules change the pattern, all to make the word easier to pronounce:

  • If the stem ends in -t / -d, an e is added for du, er and ihr: du arbeitest, er findet. Otherwise you would get a cluster like „arbeitst“.
  • If the stem ends in -s / -ß / -z, du only gets -t instead of -st: du heißt, du tanzt.
  • Verbs ending in -eln drop the e with ich: ich sammle. Verbs in -ern only add -n in the plural: wir feiern.
  • Separable verbs (shown with „|“ in the list, e.g. an|rufen): the prefix goes to the end of the sentence: Ich rufe dich an. Compare English phrasal verbs: „I call you up“.

In irregular verbs the change usually happens only with du and er/sie/es; ich and the plural forms stay regular. The vowel of the stem changes: sprechen → du sprichst, lesen → er liest, fahren → du fährst. sein, haben, werden, wissen and the modal verbs (können, müssen, wollen, mögen, möchten) have their own patterns and need to be learned one by one.

Saati sormak: Wie spät ist es? / Wie viel Uhr ist es? Ne zaman? Wann?

Günlük konuşmada (inoffiziell) 12 saatlik düzen ve nach / vor / halb kullanılıyor:

2:45Viertel vor drei

SayılarK1 K2

Grammar summary

The grammar points from the „kurz und klar“ pages at the end of each unit, grouped by topic. Tap a heading to open it; the tag shows which unit it comes from.

Personal pronouns and verb conjugationK1 K2

The verb takes an ending that matches the subject (ich, du, er …). The -en of the infinitive is dropped and the person's ending is added to the stem:

ichwohnewirwohnen
duwohnstihrwohnt
er/sie/eswohntsie/Siewohnen

Lower-case sie means both „she“ and „they“; the verb tells you which: sie wohnt (she), sie wohnen (they). Capital Sie is the polite „you“ and is conjugated like „they“. du is the informal „you“ for one person, ihr for several people.

The two most frequent irregular verbs:

seinhaben
ichbinhabe
dubisthast
er/sie/esisthat
wirsindhaben
ihrseidhabt
sie/Siesindhaben

In a text, once a person has been named, you continue with a pronoun: Das ist Frau Lang. Sie wohnt in Frankfurt. Das ist Jan. Er kommt aus Frankfurt.

All verbs with their conjugation and spelling rules are in the Verbs tab.

Statements: the verb in position 2K1 K4

The one fixed rule of a German statement (Aussagesatz): the conjugated verb is always in position 2. Position 1 can be the subject or any other element. If the subject is not first, it moves directly behind the verb:

Linaisstmorgens Müsli.
MorgensisstLina Müsli.
Am Montagarbeiteich.

A time expression like „morgens“ counts as one position on its own. Unlike English, the subject cannot stay in front of the verb: „Morgens Lina isst“ (in the morning Lina eats) is wrong.

QuestionsK1 K2

W-Frage (question with a question word): question word in position 1, verb in position 2, subject after the verb. There is no helper verb like English „do“.

Question words in the book: wer (who), was (what), wie (how), wo (where), woher (where from), wohin (where to), wann (when), welche (which), wie viel (how much), wie lange (how long).

Ja-/Nein-Frage (yes/no question): no question word, the verb moves to the front, the answer is ja or nein.

Articles: der / ein / keinK2 K3

Every noun has a gender: masculine (der), neuter (das), feminine (die). The plural is always die.

unbestimmtbestimmtNegation
masculineein Hafender Hafenkein Bahnhof
neuterein Hoteldas Hotelkein Rathaus
feminineeine Brückedie Brückekeine Straße
Plural– Schiffedie Schiffekeine Autos

Just like English „a“ and „the“: ein/eine for something new or not yet known: Das ist ein Hafen. der/das/die for something known or specific: Das ist der Hafen von Hamburg. There is no indefinite article in the plural (English: „ships“, not „a ships“).

kein is the negative of ein: „not a / no“. It negates nouns: Das ist kein Bus. To negate a verb or the whole sentence, use nicht: Das geht nicht. Ich esse nicht gern Fisch.

PluralK2

There is no single plural rule; each noun's plural is learned together with its article. The five types in the book:

(¨)-der Kilometer → die Kilometer
-(e)ndie Stunde → die Stunden
(¨)-eder Tag → die Tage, der Arzt → die Ärzte
(¨)-erdas Buch → die Bücher
-sdas Auto → die Autos

The (¨) sign means that in some words a, o, u in the stem become ä, ö, ü – much like English foot → feet, man → men. The article in the plural is always die.

Nominativ and AkkusativK4

The subject of the sentence (who does something) is in the Nominativ; the direct object (what is affected) is in the Akkusativ. English shows this only with pronouns (he → him): Er kauft den Käse. Ich sehe ihn. German shows it on the article.

NominativAkkusativ
masculineder/ein/kein Käseden/einen/keinen Käse
neuterdas/ein/kein Brotdas/ein/kein Brot
femininedie/eine/keine Gurkedie/eine/keine Gurke
Pluraldie/–/keine Tomatendie/–/keine Tomaten

The only change in the table is in the masculine: der → den, ein → einen, kein → keinen. Neuter, feminine and plural stay the same.

Verbs with Akkusativ in the book: brauchen, haben, machen, kochen, essen, kaufen, nehmen, mögen, möchten. Examples: Wir haben keinen Käse. Wir machen einen Salat. Ich nehme den Schinken.

sein does not take the Akkusativ: Das ist der Käse (not den).

gern, mögen, möchtenK2 K4

All three are about liking or wanting, but they are different structures:

  • verb + gern: to like doing something. Ich reise gern (I like travelling). Hörst du gern Musik? Negative: nicht (so) gern.
  • mögen + noun: to like something. Ich mag Obst. Ich mag keinen Fisch. Irregular: ich mag, du magst, er mag.
  • möchten: polite wish, „would like“. Ich möchte ein Stück Emmentaler, bitte. Was möchten Sie?

As in English, the owner decides: his = sein, her = ihr – even before a masculine noun: Anna und ihr Vater. When euer takes an ending, the middle e drops: eure Eltern. ihr means both „her“ and „their“; capital Ihr is the polite „your“.

Modalverben and SatzklammerK5

The book has three modal verbs: können (can, be able to), müssen (must, have to), wollen (want to, intend to). In the singular the vowel changes, and ich and er have the same form without an ending:

müssenkönnenwollen
ichmusskannwill
dumusstkannstwillst
er/es/siemusskannwill
wirmüssenkönnenwollen
ihrmüsstkönntwollt
sie/Siemüssenkönnenwollen

Careful: German will means „want to“, not the English future „will“.

Satzklammer: the modal verb is conjugated and stays in its usual place (position 2 in a statement, position 1 in a yes/no question); the main verb goes to the very end in the infinitive. Together they frame the sentence like a bracket. In English the two verbs stay together („I can go“); in German they split.

Telling the timeK5

Asking the time: Wie spät ist es? / Wie viel Uhr ist es? When? Wann?

In everyday speech (inoffiziell) the 12-hour clock is used with nach (past) / vor (to) / halb:

9:10zehn nach neun
2:45Viertel vor drei
1:30halb zwei

halb refers to the coming hour: halb zwei = 1:30 („half to two“), not 2:30. This is the point English speakers mix up most, because English says „half past one“.

Official (offiziell) 24-hour time, hours first and then minutes: 14:45 = vierzehn Uhr fünfundvierzig. 9:10 = neun Uhr zehn.

Time prepositions:

amdays, parts of the dayam Montag, am Vormittag
umclock timeum Viertel vor drei, um 17 Uhr
von … bisdurationvon Montag bis Samstag, von neun bis halb zwei

Wann? is answered with am/um, Wie lange? with von … bis. Compare English: on Monday → am Montag, at three → um drei, from … to/until → von … bis.

NumbersK1 K2

From 21 to 99 the units come first, then und, then the tens: 21 = einundzwanzig („one and twenty“), 45 = fünfundvierzig – like the old English „four-and-twenty“.

Spellings to watch: 16 sechzehn (the s drops), 17 siebzehn (the en drops), 30 dreißig, 60 sechzig, 70 siebzig.

Long numbers are written as one word: 4.520 = viertausendfünfhundertzwanzig. 1.000.000 = eine Million, 1.000.000.000 = eine Milliarde (an English „billion“). Note that German uses a full stop to group thousands and a comma for decimals.
